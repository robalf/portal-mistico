Beaver Supermoon 2026: What Happened, What It Means and What’s Next

By Roberto Rodriguez · November 25, 2026 · 6 min read

If you looked up on Monday night, November 23, or Tuesday night, November 24, and the Moon seemed a little bigger than usual, it wasn't your imagination, but it wasn't dramatic either. The Beaver Supermoon of 2026 was a great excuse to step outside rather than a spectacle that left anyone speechless. If you missed it, don't worry: the next full moon, on December 23–24, is even closer to Earth. Here's what happened, why it's called the Beaver Moon, what it means symbolically and how to make the most of the next one.

What happened on November 24, 2026

The Moon was technically full on Tuesday, November 24, at about 14:53 UTC. In U.S. time zones that was 9:53 a.m. EST, 8:53 a.m. CST, 7:53 a.m. MST and 6:53 a.m. PST; in London it was 2:53 p.m. Because the exact moment fell in daylight across North America, the best views were on the nights of the 23rd and the 24th, when the Moon looked essentially full. Tonight (November 25) it is still more than 95% lit, so a late look still counts.

It counted as a supermoon because it lined up with perigee, the part of the Moon's orbit where it comes closest to Earth. According to Star Walk, the Moon was about 360,768 km (roughly 224,170 miles) away at full phase, compared with an average of 384,400 km (238,855 miles). Our own calculation with Astronomy Engine gives practically the same figure. That made it about 6.5% larger in apparent diameter and around 13.5% brighter than an average full moon. To the naked eye, the difference is subtle. To put it plainly: unless you compared it side by side with another full moon, you probably didn't notice.

The next one is bigger: the Cold Moon on December 23–24

November's supermoon was not the biggest of the year. December's full moon, the Cold Moon, peaks at 01:29 UTC on December 24, which is the evening of December 23 in the Americas (8:29 p.m. EST, 5:29 p.m. PST). The Moon will be about 356,700 km away, roughly 4,000 km closer than in November, making it the largest full moon of 2026. If you missed the Beaver Moon, put that evening in your calendar: it falls right before Christmas Eve, a perfect excuse for a short family walk after dinner.

Why it's called the Beaver Moon

The name comes from the North American tradition collected by the Old Farmer's Almanac. Two explanations live side by side. One says November was when beavers reinforced their lodges and stored food before winter. The other says it was the season to set beaver traps before rivers and wetlands froze over. Names varied between Indigenous nations and colonial settlers, so it's best to treat it as a cultural custom rather than an astronomical rule.

There's a nuance that often gets lost: in the Southern Hemisphere, November is spring, and the image of preparing for the cold doesn't fit at all. It's a name borrowed from a northern climate. That doesn't make it wrong, but if you're reading this from Australia or Argentina, the symbolism needs a little translation.

What was in the sky: the Pleiades and Uranus

The Moon sat in the constellation Taurus, very close to the Pleiades star cluster (the Seven Sisters). According to Star Walk, around 12:05 UTC on November 24 it passed almost directly over Alcyone, the brightest star in the group, and from parts of East Asia, the Pacific and North America the Moon covered some of the cluster's stars, an event called an occultation. Uranus was about 5 degrees away, but far too faint to see easily next to a bright Moon. The pairing of Moon and Pleiades repeats every month for a while, so binoculars on a clear night near the next full moon are worth a try.

What it means symbolically

In astrology, this full moon fell in Gemini, directly opposite the Sun, which entered Sagittarius on November 22. Tradition reads the Gemini–Sagittarius axis as information versus meaning: lots of talk, lots of data, lots of options, set against the question of which belief or direction actually holds you up. It's not a prediction; it's a way to organize the conversation you have with yourself.

In almost every tradition, the full moon symbolizes culmination, and the days after it, the waning phase, are associated with letting go. The beaver adds a concrete image: getting your shelter ready. Symbolically, this week is a good time to look at what you built this year, what you're ready to stop carrying and what you need to reinforce before the end of December. None of this changes what will happen in your life; at most, it helps you think with a bit more calm.

A simple end-of-cycle ritual for this week

What follows is a symbolic suggestion, not a technique with guaranteed results. It takes about ten minutes, and the waning days after a full moon are traditionally a good fit.

  1. Write down three things you finished or let go of this year. They can be small: a habit, an argument, a project.
  2. Write down one thing you want to strengthen (your rest, a friendship, your household budget, anything) and a first concrete step for December.
  3. Step outside and look at the Moon, or simply lean out of the window. Read what you wrote out loud, without drama.
  4. Keep the page and read it again at the December 23 full moon. That's my favorite part: it comes with a review date.

If you'd rather keep it even simpler, switching off your screens for ten minutes and looking at the sky works just as well as any other ritual, and probably better than many. The site's daily moon ritual suggests a short gesture for each phase if you'd like more ideas.

Tips for the December supermoon

  • Watch the moonrise. Near the horizon the Moon looks bigger (a well-known optical illusion), and with a supermoon the effect is even more striking.
  • Find a clear eastern horizon, without buildings or trees in the way.
  • Bring binoculars if you have them. Craters along the edge of the lit disk stand out much better.
  • Check your local times with the moon phase today tool.
  • Dress for the night. Late December evenings are cold across most of the U.S.; ten comfortable minutes beat two miserable ones.

Frequently asked questions

Did the supermoon really look bigger?

Yes, about 6.5% larger in apparent diameter than an average full moon, according to Star Walk, but it was hard to notice with the naked eye.

What time was the full moon?

The peak was at 14:53 UTC on November 24, 2026: 9:53 a.m. EST, 8:53 a.m. CST, 7:53 a.m. MST and 6:53 a.m. PST. In the U.S. it happened in daylight, so the best views were on the nights of the 23rd and 24th.

When is the next supermoon?

The Cold Moon peaks at 01:29 UTC on December 24, 2026, which is the evening of December 23 in the Americas. It is the closest and largest full moon of 2026.

Does a supermoon affect my life or my mood?

There is no scientific evidence that it does. Symbolically it is used as a moment to pause and take stock; treat it as a reflection ritual, not a prediction.
