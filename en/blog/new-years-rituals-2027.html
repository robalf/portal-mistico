New Year's Rituals 2027 Around the World: Lucky Foods, Colors and Customs

By Roberto Rodriguez · November 23, 2026 · 10 min read

In the United States, New Year’s Eve often looks like a countdown on TV, a glass of champagne, a kiss at midnight and a list of resolutions that may or may not survive January. That is already a ritual, even if we rarely call it that. In 2027 it sits next to a numerology detail worth knowing: 2027 is a universal year 11 (2 + 0 + 2 + 7 = 11), a master number associated with intuition and a slightly raw nerves. It is not a promise. It is a tone. If you want the number that actually applies to you, use the 2027 personal year calculator.

Plenty of U.S. households already mix traditions. Southern families cook black-eyed peas and greens on New Year’s Day. Italian American tables bring out lentils. In Latino communities — from Los Angeles to Miami, New York, Texas and Puerto Rico — the night includes twelve grapes, yellow or red underwear and a suitcase walked around the block. Below is a practical guide to those customs and to a few from Spain, Brazil, Italy, Scotland, Japan and the Philippines that travel well, without turning midnight into a checklist.

Universal year 11: the 2027 calculation

Western numerology adds the digits of the calendar year: 2 + 0 + 2 + 7 = 11. Eleven is a master number, so the usual reading does not reduce it to 2. If you do reduce it, you get a 2 — cooperation, patience, the slower work of pairs — but the year still carries the 11’s edge: insight, intensity, and a habit of taking on too much at once.

When to do the rituals

Most people use local midnight on the night of Thursday, December 31, 2026, into Friday, January 1, 2027. If you watch the Times Square ball drop from the West Coast, remember that New York hits midnight three hours earlier; the custom still works at your own 12:00. Spain eats its grapes to the clock at Madrid’s Puerta del Sol, which is six hours ahead of the U.S. Eastern time in winter. You can watch that broadcast for fun and still do your grapes at local midnight.

The day leading up to it matters more than the exact second. Take out the trash, finish a message you have been postponing, and clear a corner of the table for fruit, a pot of peas or a glass. How to do the rituals Americans use most

A toast, a kiss, a resolution that can survive January

The U.S. midnight toast is simple: be with people you can stand at 12:01, say the year out loud, and keep the resolution small enough to test in the first week. In a year 11, “I will notice what I actually want” beats a bloated list. The kiss is optional and should be welcome, not a script. If you are alone, a raised glass still marks the cut between years.

Black-eyed peas, greens and cornbread

On January 1, Southern tables serve Hoppin’ John or a bowl of black-eyed peas (coins), greens such as collards or kale (paper money) and cornbread (gold). Eat a modest plate; the symbolism is in sitting down to it, not in finishing a pot. Some families add pork. This is folk custom, not a financial plan. Leftovers the next day are considered a good sign in many houses: the abundance did not run out on the first morning.

Twelve grapes at midnight

The custom started in Spain and is now common across Latin America and in Hispanic homes in the United States. You eat twelve grapes on the twelve strokes of midnight, one wish per month. Peel them or snip them ahead of time; the usual failure is waiting until the countdown and then choking on a rush. If you cannot eat grapes, twelve raisins or twelve small pieces of round fruit do the same job. Put the plate out before the party starts. Midnight arrives faster than the last conversation.

Yellow or red underwear, and the suitcase

Yellow is for work and money; red is for love. The garment goes on before midnight and stays on into January 1. It does not have to be new, though many people buy a fresh pair to make the cut visible. Right after 12:00, some families walk around the block — or down the hallway — with a suitcase, thinking of a trip or a move they actually want, not a vague “more travel.” If you cannot go outside, open the door, take a few steps, and come back in with the same idea.

New Year’s Day folk rules (optional)

Some American families treat January 1 as carefully as the night before. A common folk rule is not to wash clothes that day, so you do not “wash away” the luck you just invited. Another is to take something out of the house only after you have brought something in — even a loaf of bread or a sprig of green. None of this is mandatory. If a rule makes the day anxious, drop it. The point of a first-day custom is to start with a little intention, not a stack of superstitions you do not believe.

Rituals from Latin America, the Caribbean and Brazil

In Mexico, Colombia, Venezuela, Peru, Chile, Argentina and Ecuador, the same four cábalas show up in different combinations: grapes, yellow or red clothes, lentils and the suitcase. In Colombia and Ecuador — and in parts of Venezuela and Peru — people also build an año viejo, a stuffed effigy filled with notes of what they want to leave behind, and burn it at midnight. If you try a version of that in the U.S., use a fire-safe container or simply tear the paper and throw it away. City rules and dry winter weather are not a minor detail.

In Cuba, families throw a bucket of water out the door to wash the old year into the street — do this with care on ice or a shared hallway. In the Dominican Republic and Puerto Rico, the house is cleaned thoroughly, the grapes and yellow clothes come out, and the table stays full. Puerto Rican Media Noche often includes roast pork and arroz con gandules, which plays the same role as lentils: small food, large table. Brazil is its own night. People wear white for peace, add gold or yellow for prosperity, and avoid black. On the beach they jump seven waves, one wish per wave, sometimes with flowers for Iemanjá, and step into the year with the right foot. No ocean nearby? Seven deliberate steps down a hall will do.

Europe and Asia: rituals that travel

Spain and Italy

Spain’s grapes are the source of the Latin American custom, timed to the Puerta del Sol clock. Italy puts the emphasis on food: lentils with cotechino or zampone around midnight, each spoonful read as a coin, and red underwear for love. Throwing old furniture out the window is a story from another century; skip it. A pot of plain lentils is enough if you cannot find cotechino at an Italian market.

Scotland and Denmark

Scottish Hogmanay includes cleaning the house (redding) and first-footing: the first person over the threshold after midnight should, in the older telling, be a dark-haired man bringing coal, whisky, shortbread or salt. Today it is usually a friend who arrives with something to offer, not empty-handed. Auld Lang Syne, which American crowds already sing, comes from a Robert Burns poem. In Denmark, people smash old plates against a friend’s door — a larger pile of shards means you are well liked — and at midnight many jump off a chair into the new year. If you borrow the jump, warn anyone in the house and leave the good dishes in the cabinet.

Japan and the Philippines

Japanese New Year (Oshogatsu) falls on January 1, not on Lunar New Year. Families clean the house (osoji), eat toshikoshi soba to “cross” the year, and temples ring the bell 108 times at midnight (joya no kane). The first shrine or temple visit (hatsumode) comes in the days that follow. One calendar warning: casual Japanese almanacs often name the year’s zodiac animal from January 1, but in Chinese astrology the Fire Goat year begins on February 6, 2027. They are different clocks.

In the Philippines, Media Noche calls for twelve round fruits — oranges, grapes, apples, melon — because the circle stands for coins and for a year that does not break. Polka dots and other round patterns on clothes follow the same idea. Children jump at midnight “to grow.” If you cannot find twelve kinds of fruit, twelve round pieces, even if some are repeats, keep the symbol intact.

A simple plan for New Year’s Eve 2027

You do not need every custom on this page. Pick the ones that already live in your family, or one borrowed rite that you can do without a trip to a specialty store. A plan that holds up: one abundance food on January 1, one color or garment chosen on purpose, and one small closing gesture at midnight — grapes, a torn piece of paper, a short walk, a raised glass. Year 11 gets noisy when the wish list gets long.

Frequently asked questions

What time should I do New Year's rituals in 2027?

Most people use local midnight on the night of December 31, 2026 into January 1, 2027. If you watch the Times Square ball drop from another time zone, still do your grapes, toast or walk at your own 12:00.

Why is 2027 a universal year 11?

You add the digits of the calendar year: 2 + 0 + 2 + 7 = 11. Eleven is a master number, so the usual reading does not reduce it to 2. Your personal year is a different number, based on your birth month and day plus 2027.

Can I combine several rituals on the same night?

Yes, but you do not need all of them. A plan that holds up is one abundance food, one color or garment chosen on purpose, and one small midnight gesture such as grapes, a short walk or a raised glass.

Does lucky underwear have to be new?

No. Many people buy a fresh pair to mark the cut between years, but a garment you already own works if you put it on before midnight and wear it into January 1. Yellow is usually for work and money; red is for love.

What if I cannot eat grapes or black-eyed peas?

Twelve raisins or twelve pieces of round fruit can stand in for the grapes. A small plate of lentils, pigeon peas or another modest dish can take the place of black-eyed peas. The symbol is a first-day table that is not empty, not a single ingredient.

Do these rituals actually work?

They are popular traditions and symbolic gestures for closing the year with an intention. They do not guarantee money, love or travel, and they do not replace practical decisions or professional advice.
